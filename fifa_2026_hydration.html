FIFA 2026: Hydration Breaks & Match Performance

Project Overview

The FIFA World Cup is the biggest sporting event on the planet, breaking viewership and social engagement records.

During its latest edition in June and July 2026, controversy arose over the use of hydration breaks for commercial advertising. While companies found a new window to advertise their products, fans strongly resented the pauses that disrupted the flow of the game, with audible boos heard in several matches whenever hydration breaks were whistled. For coaching staffs, these intervals were capitalized on to deliver tactical adjustments. As for the players—the primary reason for the breaks—they took advantage of the moments to rehydrate and restore energy while taking in tactical instructions.

Portugal v Uzbekistan, played in an air-conditioned stadium, is paused for a hydration break. Photograph: Chris Brunskill/Fantasista/Getty Images

As a football fan, I followed the tournament closely, and the fact that goals were scored immediately after hydration breaks (e.g., USA vs. Paraguay, Australia vs. Turkey, or Germany vs. Curaçao) sparked debate. This prompted questions about whether the breaks truly altered the game's dynamics, which motivated me to seek answers through statistical analysis of the competition’s data.

This curiosity led me to investigate whether any differences existed in match variables following hydration breaks. To that end, gathering relevant data was essential. However, the data collection phase was challenging, as the data officially published by FIFA was limited to key match events.

Fortunately, I found a project developed by Jose David Chiquillo, who collected a real-time dataset across the entire competition, creating a detailed chronological record of each match.

This data includes game state snapshots (every 30 seconds): score evolution, statistics, and match phases over time; live positional data (every 5 seconds): ball coordinates and ongoing game situations; and discrete events: specific records of goals, cards, and substitutions.

During the data analysis, although there was no direct reference to hydration break timestamps, the following iteration was performed on each match to identify those moments: between minutes 20–30 and minutes 65–75, check whether a stoppage lasting longer than 2 minutes occurred (breaks were scheduled around the 22nd minute of each half with an expected duration of 3 minutes). If only a single result was returned within that window, it was identified as the hydration break; otherwise, another prolonged interruption might have occurred—such as a player injury—requiring cross-referencing with other sources.

Out of a total of 104 matches, one game lacked recorded data (Brazil vs. Norway). Across the remaining 103 matches, hydration breaks meeting the previously described criteria were identified in both halves, and each pause was adjusted to its actual duration along with its start and end minutes. Exceptions and uncertainties arose only in the following fixtures:

  • Mexico vs. South Africa: the second-half break lasted only 1:35 (69:01–70:36).
  • France vs. Iraq: there were no break markers and no stoppage exceeding 83 seconds.
  • Spain vs. Cape Verde: the feed contained no events between 17:30 and 26:46, recording only the “gameon” event at 26:46.

Sample of the analyzed matches with identified hydration break start, end, and duration timestamps.

In the Mexico vs. South Africa match, external information revealed that the second-half hydration break took place immediately following a goal, which curiously sparked controversy as broadcast commercials ran longer than expected.

In the France vs. Iraq match, a kickoff delay occurred before the game, and it was decided that the second half would not feature a hydration break.

In the Spain vs. Cape Verde match, consulting detailed match commentary was required to confirm that the first-half hydration break began at minute 23.
